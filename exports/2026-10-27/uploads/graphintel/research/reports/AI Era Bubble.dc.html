Companion B to the Seeded Substrate paper/Macro argument/15 October 2026

The dot-com bubble of the AI era

The argument: three years of GPU build-out, increasingly financed with debt, is being priced on scarce, expensive intelligence. Cheap, democratised access to AI undercuts that price while it squeezes entry-level work. If GPU demand breaks, the losses fall on a credit-funded sector that now props up growth, at the same time as hiring thins. This report sets out the case for reading that as an early warning of a depression-scale downturn, the evidence against it, and the indicators that would settle it.

01 · Why this sits beside the substrate paper

The paper's H0 says model size stops mattering once the cognitive layer is seeded. If that is true at scale, it is also an economic claim: the value assumed in the GPU build-out sits in the wrong layer. This report follows that claim to the macro picture. Anima is one of the forces it describes, so its interest in the conclusion should be read with that in mind.

04 · The case against

What the evidence does not show yet

Smaller than the dot-com surge

The IMF puts the AI boom's added investment at about 0.4% of US GDP since 2022, against roughly 1.2% for internet investment in 1995–2000, and notes the spenders are cash-rich (Tech Times, 14 Oct).

No aggregate unemployment signal

Anthropic's March 2026 study found no detectable rise in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. A CESifo paper found recent-graduate unemployment in summer 2026 within its 2022–26 range (Digital Applied; BigGo).

Within historical norms

Goldman projects AI capex at 1.8% of GDP in 2026 rising to 2.8% in 2028, inside the 2–5% range of earlier general-purpose technology build-outs (AI Eating the World).

The labour effect so far is fewer hires, not layoffs, and the Stanford authors describe it as an early indicator rather than a causal estimate. The strongest form of the argument is about what happens if links 2 and 6 move together, not about what has happened.

06 · Reading

A sectoral correction is likely; a depression needs more to go wrong

The evidence supports a dot-com-style correction in AI infrastructure: a revenue gap acknowledged by Gartner, falling unit prices, rising leverage and prices that move hard on efficiency news. A depression would need those losses to reach the credit system and coincide with a broad fall in employment. Neither has happened. Two parts of the chain are new compared with 2000: the build-out is now partly debt-funded, and the same technology is displacing entry-level work. Those are the two indicators to watch.

For the institute, the business case in Blueprint v3 does not depend on a crash. It depends on buyers preferring quality per dollar, which they do in either scenario.
