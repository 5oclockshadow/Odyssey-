MINA, 6 · SITTING WITH A PARENT

“Can I make a new one?”

WHAT SHE DOES

Taps a ball on the picture and asks for a different one. The green ball turns pale blue. The picture is still a picture of something real.

WHAT THE SCREEN SAYS

Four ticks and one empty circle. Underneath, in her words: the balls can hold hands properly, nobody has written this one down before, nothing on it is the dangerous kind, and it is the right size and weight for a medicine.

WHAT SHE TAKES AWAY

She drew something nobody has drawn. That is true, and it is the part worth being pleased about.

WHAT IT MUST NOT SAY

That she made a medicine. The empty circle is the whole point: no one has made this, held it, or given it to anybody. A person in a lab has to do that, and it takes years.

For her the five steps are a game with one honest rule: the last circle never fills in by itself.
FIRST-YEAR CHEMISTRY PHD · MIT

“What exactly did it check?”

WHAT HE DOES

Pastes a SMILES string, asks for analogs, and reads the ladder as five separate assertions rather than one verdict.

WHAT THE SCREEN SAYS

Valid is RDKit parsing and sanitising the structure. New is an exact-structure miss in PubChem. Stable is a SMARTS screen for alert groups — peroxide, azide, acyl halide and the rest. Drug-like is a weight, logP and hydrogen-bond count. Backed needs attached literature.

WHAT HE TAKES AWAY

A filter, not a result. Para-Cl to para-F is a standard bioisosteric swap; the interesting question is what it does to the ring's electronics and to metabolic clearance, and the tool has not touched either.

WHAT IT MUST NOT SAY

That the compound is stable or synthesisable. The alert screen is the absence of known-bad substructures, which is not thermodynamics. There is no retrosynthesis, no docking, no ADMET model and no conformer anywhere in the pipeline.

He is the reader who can break it, and the one the honest labels are written for: every level names the test it ran, so he can disagree with the test.
PATENT LAW SEMINAR · SECOND YEAR

“New to whom?”

WHAT THEY DO

Read one word on the screen — New — and ask what database it was measured against, and on what date.

WHAT THE SCREEN SAYS

That this exact structure is not indexed in PubChem. The run log keeps the query, the timestamp and the source for every claim on the map, which is the part that survives being questioned later.

WHAT THEY TAKE AWAY

One database's silence, dated. Useful as a starting screen and as a record of what was known when.

WHAT IT MUST NOT SAY

Novel, patentable, or clear to use. A public chemical index is not a prior-art search: it does not reach patents and applications, unpublished filings, private libraries, or anything a person could argue is an obvious variation of what already exists. A single-atom swap on a known scaffold is exactly the fact pattern where that argument gets made.

They are why the label reads New to PubChem and not New. The qualifier is the claim.
NEUROSURGEON · BETWEEN CASES

“Does it reach the tissue, and does it harm it?”

WHAT SHE DOES

Skips the structure entirely and looks for the target, the route of administration, and whether anything on the map came from a trial.

WHAT THE SCREEN SAYS

The papers the research agents attached, each with its source, and the open questions they generated. On this run: no trial, no animal work, no measured exposure.

WHAT SHE TAKES AWAY

A literature map with the gaps marked, which is a reading tool. The one question she wants answered — whether it crosses into the tissue she operates on — is not on the ladder at all.

WHAT IT MUST NOT SAY

Anything clinical. Drug-like is a size-and-weight rule of thumb, not exposure, not selectivity, not safety. Nothing here has been near a cell, let alone a patient, and the distance between Drug-like and a first-in-human study is most of a decade.

She reads the ladder backwards: the only level she cares about is the empty one.
